# The ending

Why [book/chapter-27.md](../book/chapter-27.md) is shaped the way it is, and what the earlier
ending was doing wrong. Written while the last chapter was being rebuilt; kept as the record of
the decisions.

## What the first ending got wrong

- **The death was spent twice, and the reader was ahead both times.** Ch26 gave it to Kate via
  Claire, in full; ch27 gave it to Nik and Alex via Kate. The last chapter's central event was one
  the reader already owned. The voicemail likewise: printed in ch25, printed again in ch27.
- **Sentiment on the last page.** Empty chair, full glass, rain, fire, *same time next Friday* —
  and then a sentence explaining its own restraint.
- **Grief flattened all three registers into one narrator**, on the final page.
- **Nobody paid anything.** Every other chapter charges someone.
- **The death was a device.** Michael does every single thing the book recommends and is killed
  for it, and the ending treated him as a beloved ghost instead of asking what that costs.
- **The book's subject never got touched.** The vise is made of each other and none of the four
  ever learns it. The first ending was about friendship and loss, which is another book.

## The real cost: the telephone

Ch25: the advisory goes through in August on a single sheet — four board meetings a year and
*such counsel as may reasonably be sought by telephone.* Ch26: Kate has not rung anyone in four
months; she has been letting Tom run things, learning not to put her hand on it, dancing on
Wednesdays.

He was dying. He took a job that was a telephone. It never rang.

Not from cruelty. Because she was *better.* The cure is the mechanism. And not only hers: Alex
never rang back; Nik made the group and never wrote in it. Three people who each learned, at real
cost, to say the true thing — and not one said it to Michael. Michael is the only one who rang. He
got voicemail.

Claire delivers it in the pub as a fact, not an accusation: *he kept his phone on the table. From
August. I asked him twice what he was waiting for and he said "work", and laughed.* Then: *he had a
job at the end. Four meetings a year and a telephone. He was terribly pleased about it.*

## Claire in the fourth chair

She takes the chair. Nobody remarks on it. That is what the chair is for — not a glass.

She has come to collect the account her father promised her on the porch in ch25 and ran out of
time to give. What she gets:

- **Alex** tells the truth, which for Alex is mechanism — a bank, a ledger, a flip chart at one in
  the morning — and it is the wrong thing to say to this woman, and it dies halfway.
- **Nik** lies. *Did he talk about us?* — *Constantly.* Four per cent, which is all it ever was:
  every fact under it is real (the roof in ch11, the four seats in ch19), everything round it is
  weather. The reflex that cost him fourteen years of his marriage is, once, the only kindness in
  the room, and it works, and it is still a lie. Alex and Kate both let it stand. Nothing he says
  afterwards is funny.
- **Kate** cannot fix it and cannot leave. She stays in the chair. Her clock stops from Claire's
  arrival to the end of the pub.

All three deploy exactly what the book taught them, on a grieving daughter, and all three are
wrong.

**The voicemail as a gift.** Alex has the only thing any of them owns that Claire wants. The pub
eats most of it. What comes through whole is the joke on the end, and it lands. His jokes always
land; that is the problem with them. Alex laughs, and cannot stop, and then it is not laughing.

## Why do they keep going

The book holds two answers, one per father figure — the same sentence with opposite outcomes:

- **Michael, ch25:** *I was good at it. If I'd been worse at it I'd have been home.* He kept going
  as repayment — the sticker on the television, a promise a boy made. A debt. It killed him.
- **Marek, ch24:** *I did it because it was in front of me and there was nobody else to do it. I did
  not think about it.* Not a debt. *It is a thing that is given. You spend it.* The only person in
  the book who is not in the vise.

They keep going because they think they owe it. Kate to Harriet, whom she chose because she would
be harder on her. Nik to Chennai and the school and forty *how was work*s a week. Alex to the
bakery. None of them was ever charged. That is the vise: the next thing in front of you, done as
repayment of a debt nobody sent.

The book is finally for Marek's answer. Nobody at the table can give it.

**Claire asks it** — the only person entitled to — straight after the telephone, *the way you ask
when the trains are.* Three answers:

- **Kate:** *Because I can see it coming. If I'm not there when it lands, it lands.* Ch5's belief,
  said out loud as a reason. She hears it and lets it stand.
- **Nik:** the version he gives (school, knee, boiler) and, asked for the truer one: *It's the only
  thing anyone's ever paid me for. I'm forty-two. I don't know what I'd be on a Tuesday.* Michael's
  empty Wednesday afternoon, one generation down.
- **Alex:** *Because in a room I'm a sentence behind. Always. In the thing, I'm not. I'm ahead of
  it. It's the only place I'm ahead of anything.* The only answer at the table that is not a debt.
  *One sentence behind* is his father's phrase from ch13 — the inheritance, spent, unnoticed.
- **Michael**, last, from the dead, via Danny: *I was good at it. If I'd been worse at it I'd have
  been home.* — *Danny thought it was a joke.* Even the one that wasn't.

Then the fortnight: Danny was different for about two weeks after the step, then he wasn't. *I
don't blame him. It's his job.* The most expensive thing Michael ever did, priced.

## Two codas, two phones

The whole chapter has been phones that did not ring.

**Bow.** Alex rings his father at twenty-five to eleven with nothing to say. *I haven't got
anything. I just rang.* Ten seconds, which on that line is nothing at all. Marek: *OK. Your mother
is asleep. I am not. So.* The invitation without the word. This is the only spent inheritance in
the book — forty seconds, no content, no *it's fine, Papa*, nothing fixed. Marek gets the last
line of dialogue because he is the one who answered the question.

**Watford.** Same hour. Danny on the phone in the kitchen — the thumbs, a thread with nine people
on it. Claire in the door with her coat on. He smiles and holds up one finger. She waits. Sam on
the stairs in pyjamas, awake because of a plan, watches his father hold up the finger and his
mother wait. *Then he went back up.*

Ch25, Claire: *Sam thinks it's normal that grandparents don't come to things, and I never
corrected it. I sort of priced you out.* That is what happened to Michael — nobody stopped loving
him, they stopped waiting for him. Sam going back up is the first of Danny's empty chairs. Danny is
smiling with a finger in the air and has no idea. The vise closes on him, not on Sam — which is why
it is quiet, and why only the reader knows.

Bow before Watford: the grace note, then the cut. Ending on Marek would be consolation; ending on
Sam is the vise. Both are on the page and the book does not say which weighs more.

## Rules kept

- Characters say facts. The narrator never says what a scene meant.
- No stated moral. The one licence for saying the theme aloud was spent on Wilf.
- Registers: Nik's currency in the first section so the silence after the lie is audible; Kate's
  clock (7:03) and then none until the chapter is over; Alex's drone (the cellar cooler, the
  compressor) in every scene he is in.
- Michael alone gets no vantage on himself; he is present only as what other people report.

## Changes made alongside

- **Ch26** now ends on Kate's *"Is he—"* and the two-second silence she reads the way Nik reads a
  position. Claire's speech moved, in substance, to the pub.
- The title stays *Friday.* Everything in the chapter happens on one.

## Still open

- Plant the illness — three seeds, none explained: the chair he takes carefully on the roof (ch11),
  Ruth's face when he asks about the recital (ch19), the twenty under the glass being more than the
  drinks because he already knows he won't be back (ch20). Without them the death arrives as a
  device.

## Rejected on the way

- **A Monday coda, three registers, three wins.** Continuation is not the vise. Michael was in the
  reasoning, not on the page.
- **Alex handing a question to Fenella and putting his phone on the table.** The vise closing on
  the next engineer, Michael's habit in Alex's hand. Too built; the wrong person to end on.
- **Kate moving the Ocean call, landing on Danny.** Five inferential hops for a rescheduled
  meeting, competing with the telephone for the last position.
- **Kate alone at the window, *Michael Halloway (advisor)* in her phone.** A punchline; a punchline
  closes the door.
- **A stated moral.** Competent, and it kills the book.
