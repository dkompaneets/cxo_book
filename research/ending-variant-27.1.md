# Ending variant 27.1 — Monday

Why [book/variants/chapter-27.1.md](../book/variants/chapter-27.1.md) exists, and what it is
trying to do that [book/chapter-27.md](../book/chapter-27.md) does not. The original is untouched.

## What is wrong with the current ending

- **The death is spent twice, and the reader is ahead both times.** Ch26 gives it to Kate via
  Claire. Ch27 gives it to Nik and Alex via Kate. The last chapter's central event is one the
  reader already owns, so it lands on characters, not on the reader. The voicemail is the same:
  printed in full in ch25, printed again in ch27.
- **Sentiment on the last page.** Empty chair, full glass, rain, fire, *same time next Friday.*
  Then the prose explains its own restraint: *nobody said "to Michael", or any of the sentences
  that exist to make a moment easier.* That is a sentence telling the reader what the scene did.
- **Grief flattens all three registers into one narrator** — on the final page, which is the
  one failure the four-register rewrite exists to prevent.
- **Nobody pays anything.** Every other chapter charges someone. Ch27 is consolation.
- **The death is a device.** Michael's illness is planted nowhere before ch26. He does every
  single thing the book recommends — retires, apologises, goes to the recital, tells Danny — and
  is killed for it, and the ending treats him as a beloved ghost instead of asking what that
  costs.
- **The book's subject never gets touched.** The vise is made of each other and none of the
  four ever learns it. The current ending is about friendship and loss, which is another book.

## What the variant does

### The real cost: the telephone

Ch25: the advisory goes through in August on a single sheet — four board meetings a year and
*such counsel as may reasonably be sought by telephone.* Ch26: Kate hasn't rung anyone in four
months; she has been letting Tom run things, learning not to put her hand on it, dancing on
Wednesdays. Claire: *he said it was the first thing in years that had needed him and not the job.*

He was dying. He took a job that was a telephone. It never rang.

Not from cruelty. Because she was *better.* The cure is the mechanism. And it is not only hers:
Alex never rang back (already in the text); Nik made the group and never wrote in it. Three
people who each learned, at real cost, to say the true thing — and not one said it to Michael.
Michael is the only one who rang. He got voicemail.

Claire delivers it in the pub, as a fact, not an accusation: *he kept his phone on the table.
From August. I asked him twice what he was waiting for and he said "work", and laughed.*

### Claire in the fourth chair

She takes the chair. Nobody remarks on it. That is what the chair is for — not a glass.

She has come to collect the account her father promised her on the porch in ch25 and ran out
of time to give. What she gets:

- **Alex** tells the truth, which for Alex is mechanism — a bank, a ledger, a flip chart at one
  in the morning — and it is the wrong thing to say to this woman, and it dies halfway. His
  peak: he loses the flow.
- **Nik** lies. *Did he talk about us?* — *Constantly.* Four per cent, which is all it ever was:
  every fact under it is real (the roof in ch11, the four seats in ch19), and everything round
  it is weather. The reflex that cost him fourteen years of his marriage is, once, the only
  kindness in the room, and it works, and it is still a lie. Alex and Kate both let it stand.
  Nothing he says afterwards is funny — his peak, per his file.
- **Kate** cannot fix it and cannot leave. She stays in the chair. Her clock stops from Claire's
  arrival to the end of the pub.

All three deploy exactly what the book taught them, on a grieving daughter, and all three are
wrong. There is no version of yourself that works.

### The voicemail as a gift

Alex has the only thing any of them owns that Claire wants. The pub eats most of it. What comes
through whole is the joke on the end — *I've met your head, so, too late* — and it lands.
Canon: his jokes always land; that is the problem with them. Alex laughs, and cannot stop, and
then it is not laughing. Claire: *that's exactly him. He does a joke at the end so you can't—*

### Monday: the show goes on, and what goes on in it is him

Replaces the fourth glass and *same time next Friday.* Three short sections, three registers,
three rooms Michael will never enter. He is in all of them, named in none, and not one of the
three notices.

- **Kate**, 8:40, Harriet, Refill short. *Not a defence — a finding.* Ch8 verbatim. Out at
  9:02. The clock returns; the ostinato resumes; that is the statement.
- **Nik**, the FY27 pre-read. Priyesh asks whether January will hold. Nik answers inside a joke —
  *January's a strong candidate for March* — the room laughs, the question does not come back.
  He has inherited the working version of the thing that ruined Michael, and the thing it does.
  He wrote a memo in July to stop doing exactly this. He knows. Where he got it, he does not.
- **Alex**, call nineteen of forty. Ben comes over the top and stops himself; Alex lets six
  seconds sit — ch20, Michael: *the day you stop, it stops* — and then the eruption comes, full
  length, full joy, two minutes forty. Then the fan, and the tick, and *the next one was at four.*

### Why this spends the death at full price

Marek, ch24: *it is not a debt. You cannot pay it back. There is nobody to pay. An inheritance.
You spend it.* The death is what converts Michael from a man into three working habits in three
rooms. He is not mourned on the last page. He is used. Nobody can repay it because there is nobody
to pay, and nobody knows they are carrying it.

### Why they keep going

Michael on the step, ch25: *I was good at it. If I'd been worse at it I'd have been home.* Not
trapped. They go back because it is the best thing in their lives. Alex's eruption is, per his
file, the happiest anyone gets in this book, so the last page ends inside one. The reader feels
why he takes call twenty and nobody has to say it.

### Small relief at its true size

A Wednesday. A kitchen table. Six seconds on a call. Against forty years, four hundred closures,
a marriage nearly gone, and a dead man. Both sizes are on the page. The book does not do the
division. And none of the three is unhappy — that is the blade, and it only works unsaid.

## Last sentence and aftertaste

*The next one was at four.*

Not irony — a punchline closes the door. This leaves it open. The reader is holding a hundred and
sixty seconds of genuine joy and a queue with no end, and cannot tell whether Alex is fine or
doomed, because he is both. Michael is the reason Alex can hold six seconds of silence, he is
dead, and no one in that room will ever know his name. The aftertaste is not sadness. It is
arithmetic — the reader doing the sum on their own week, about four seconds after closing the
book, unprompted.

## Register plan

- Nik: currency on the page (£6.80, £28, £140, £94, £91,000), the mid-sentence conversion, the
  self-undercut, jokes that cost — loud in the first section so the silence after the lie is
  audible. No semicolons.
- Kate: timestamps as bar line (7:03) — then none from Claire's arrival to the end of the pub —
  then 8:40, 9:02 on Monday. Verb-first, asyndeton, full stops. No semicolons.
- Alex: the drone in every scene he is in (the cellar cooler in the pub, the rack fan on the
  call). Bimodal — room tone at five words a line, and one sentence over 200 words. The pub
  eruption dies; the Monday one runs. Non-British idiom kept light.
- Nobody explains anything. Characters say facts. The narrator does not say what a scene meant.

## Dependencies if adopted

- **Ch26 must lose its last four paragraphs**, ending on Kate's *"Is he—"* and the two-second
  silence she reads the way Nik reads a position. Otherwise the death is spent before ch27.
- **Plant the illness** — three seeds, none explained: the chair he takes carefully on the roof
  (ch11), Ruth's face when he asks about the recital (ch19), the twenty under the glass being more
  than the drinks because he already knows he won't be back (ch20).
- The title stays *Friday.* The chapter ends on Monday; the title being wrong is part of it.
- Cut the fourth-glass gesture entirely. It is not refused on the page — it simply never occurs
  to anyone, which is truer.

## Rejected on the way

- **Ocean / Danny / the Thursday call** — Kate moving the Ocean call on the last page, landing
  on Michael's son-in-law. Five inferential hops across 26 chapters for a rescheduled meeting.
  Clever, and it competed with the telephone for the final position. A puzzle where the
  strongest thing in the book should be.
- **Kate alone at the window, "Michael Halloway (advisor)" in her phone, "she slept seven
  hours."** Better, but a Kate ending on a wry note. The lift-and-lunch line was a punchline.
- **Stated moral.** *The pressure does not disappear, it only moves.* Competent, and it kills the
  book. Handing the reader's conclusion to the narrator on the last page breaks the close third
  held for 27 chapters to make a point the reader already has.
