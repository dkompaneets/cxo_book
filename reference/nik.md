# Nikhil Raghavan — comic patter

Funny, and the comedy is a cost. He is funny because funny is cheaper than plain, and every
joke is a polish. The book's dry institutional comedy is **his**, not the house's.

## Authors

- **Mick Herron** — outright. The register that used to be the whole book's is now Nik's
  alone: professional competence and rot rendered exactly, precision as the joke, the joke
  that conceals despair.
- **Joseph Heller** — the arithmetic loops. A mind running a subroutine it cannot disable.
- **Jonathan Coe** — the institutional satire underneath the domestic comedy.

## Exclusives

His and nobody else's. A property shared with another POV is a property held by none.

**Only Nik gets:**

- **The numeral.** Figures on the page, in digits, at roughly twice the rate of anyone else —
  and the *mid-sentence* conversion, always mid-sentence, never as its own sentence. The
  measured failure of the first two drafts is that Kate carried three times his numbers. She
  counts time; he counts money; nobody else counts anything.
- The running total as refrain, returned to across a chapter and never announced.
- The **self-undercut**: states a thing well, then diminishes it in the same breath. The
  verbal tic and the character wound are one mechanism.
- Deadpan understatement in the sentence immediately after a large number.

**Nik never gets:**

- **Semicolons.** Zero. They are Michael's. Nik at 5.72 per thousand was the single clearest
  sign the register had not been written — his own introduction was in Michael's voice.
- The finished paragraph. He interrupts himself; a paragraph that arrives whole is Michael's.
- Timestamps as structure. The clock is Kate's.

## Shape

Short prose, interrupted by arithmetic. Mean sentence 13–17, standard deviation 12–20, tail
8–20 per cent: tighter than Alex, looser than Kate, never in Michael's range. The variance
comes from a number breaking a sentence open, not from a long clause resolving. Currency should
run near 6 per thousand words against everybody else's 0.5 — that gap *is* the register.

`scripts/register.py 4` checks this per chapter.

## Sense channel

**He sees prices.** Menus, tariff cards, the cost per hour of the thing he is currently doing.
Where Michael smells buildings and Alex hears machines, Nik cannot look at an object without
its number arriving. Use it for comedy first and grief second, in that order, always.

## Prosody

- **Involuntary conversion.** He cannot stop doing arithmetic, and the parenthetical
  conversion goes mid-sentence, always. The hotel tariff card becoming two-thirds of a term's
  school fees is the funniest thing about him and it is currently played straight.
- Comic precision — exact figures where a vague one would do.

## At the peak

He loses the jokes. Nothing else changes — same sentence length, same precision — but the
comedy stops dead, and the silence where it was is the loudest thing in the chapter.

**This only works if he is noisy first.** Making him funny in ch1–18 is what makes ch23 land.
As drafted you cannot hear the silence, because he was never making a sound.

## In the current draft

His strongest chapters are 18 (the four spreadsheet runs, the bounce landing at 8:56) and 23
(Meera's *"I know"*, Rohan pointing at the chair). Ch7 gives him Wilf, who is the best minor
character in the book.

The problem to fix: he scores lowest of the four on comedy in his own chapters. The funniest
character in the book is currently the saddest, and the comedy has all been assigned to
Michael. That inversion is a large part of why the four voices collapse into one.

His chapters: 4, 7, 13 (with Alex), 18, 23. Sections in 5, 9, 10, 11, 21.

## Watch for

- **No contempt.** This matters more for Nik than for anyone. A sardonic narrator curdles him
  into caricature immediately — and the same applies to Marek in ch13, whose dialect must not
  do work the prose should be doing.
- Comedy that costs him nothing. If a joke is free, cut it or make him pay.
- The accent and the four seconds are his wound, not his voice. Never render them as broken
  English on the page.
