# 13. The Old Country

The lobby of The Ardenne had a note and the note was the doors.

Two sets of them, automatic, four metres apart, and every arrival opened both and let the car park in for a second and a half. Under that, the plant. Above the desk, nine hundred glass rods on wires, doing nothing, ticking very faintly against each other when the doors went.

They arrived at twenty past ten on the Saturday morning.

They had taken a train from Euston at 7:41, to be safe. Then a second train, to be safe. Then a taxi. Travelling since a quarter past six, for a journey of ninety minutes.

Ewa Wójcik was wearing the coat.

"Mama, it's twenty-four degrees."

"It's a coat for arriving," said his mother.

Marek Wójcik was sixty-eight and had a bad shoulder from the print works and carried both bags. When Alex tried to take one there was a short, complete, wordless struggle in the middle of the lobby, which Marek won, as he had won it every time since 2009.

They checked in. It took a while.

Ewa wanted to know about breakfast, and then wanted to know about breakfast again in a different way. Marek stood four feet back from the desk with the bags and watched the transaction with the total unblinking attention of a man who has been overcharged in three countries.

Then Ewa went up to look at the room and to unpack, which would take forty minutes and could not be hurried.

Alex and his father sat down in two chairs by the fireplace with no fire in it.

"So," said Marek.

"So."

"It's a big hotel."

"It's a big hotel."

His father looked up at the glass rods for a while.

"Twenty-nine pounds," he said.

"There was an offer."

"Mm," said Marek.

---

Nik came through the lobby at eleven on his way to the atrium, and Alex did the thing you do, the half-lift of the hand that gives the other person a way out.

Nik did not take the way out. He came over.

"Round three's at two," he said, "so I'm free for exactly two hours and then I'm no use to anybody."

"This is my dad. Papa — this is Nik. He beat me at chess in nineteen moves."

Marek Wójcik stood up, which took him a second, and shook hands.

"Nineteen. That's good. When he was ten, if it went past thirty he was crying."

"*Papa.*"

"It's true."

Forty seconds, and the two of them had found it.

Alex sat and watched it happen and could not have said afterwards where the turn was. Somewhere in the first few minutes his father said *when we came*, and Nik said *when did you come*, and Marek said 1994, and Nik said 1999, and there was a small pause. And then the two of them were off into a country Alex had lived beside his whole life and had never once been able to enter.

They did not talk about hardship.

He had assumed, insofar as he had ever imagined this conversation at all, that it would be a comparing of wounds.

It was almost all logistics, and it was very funny.

They talked about the first flat. Marek's had been in Leyton, two rooms, with a meter that took fifty-pence pieces, and the landlord had been a man called Costas who was, Marek said, "not a bad man. But always he is thinking I steal the fifty pence. From my own meter." Nik's had been his uncle's box room in Leicester, with a wardrobe he could not fully open because of the bed.

They talked about the documents. Both of them, it emerged, had a plastic wallet. Marek's was blue and lived in a drawer in the kitchen and had done for thirty-one years, and contained every piece of paper the Home Office had ever sent him, in order, including the envelopes. Nik's was clear and lived in a fireproof box. When he admitted the fireproof box, Marek nodded slowly and said, "Yes. Yes. I should think about this," in the tone of a man conceding a technical point.

And they talked about the language.

"The words were fine," Marek said. "Words you learn." He stopped, and made a small quick circling gesture with two fingers, and did not find what he wanted, and went on without it. "It's the speed. They talk, and you have the words, all of them, every one, and then they finish — and you are still one sentence behind."

He turned his hand over.

"And then they look."

"And then they look," said Nik.

"So you say nothing."

"So you say nothing."

"For me, six years." Marek shrugged with the good shoulder. "At the works there was a man, Tony. He thought there was something wrong with me. Six years he thought this. Then one day the machine breaks and I fix it, and everybody is standing there, and Tony says: *Marek, you're a dark horse.*" A pause. "Twenty years we were friends. He came to Alek's christening. But six years."

---

Nik watched him.

He watched him for the whole two hours, in fact, and would have been unable to explain why he did not go and prepare for round three.

Marek Wójcik was sixty-eight. He had come over at thirty-seven with a wife, no English, and a trade that did not transfer. He had done nights at a bakery on Bethnal Green Road and days at a print works for ten years — which Nik had done the arithmetic on within about ninety seconds of hearing it, ten years of four hours' sleep, and had then done again as a total, and had then stopped. He had bought a flat in 2006 and paid it off in 2021, and was now retired, and had a bad shoulder, and had come to Birmingham on two trains to see his son for a weekend.

And he was *calm.*

That was the thing. Nik kept looking for it and could not find it. He knew what he was looking for, because he carried it: the low hum under everything, the running total, the eye that goes to the price on the menu first.

He had assumed, without ever putting it into a sentence, that the hum was simply what this life did to a man. That in thirty years he would be a version of himself with more money and the same hum. That this was the deal.

Marek Wójcik did not have the hum.

He had an opinion about the coffee. He had a long and detailed complaint about a train company. He asked Nik four questions about Chennai and listened to all four answers. And when Ewa came down and said the room was fine but the pillows were "like a paper", he said, "So we ask for another pillow" — and Nik watched a man solve a problem in six words at a volume of about thirty per cent, and understood, and covered the understanding by drinking some water.

This is what it looks like when the door has actually been shut, and the room behind it is actually empty, and the man has walked away from it.

*The fear ends*, Nik thought.

Not: the fear was never justified. It had been justified. Six years of saying nothing at a print works in Leyton is not a misunderstanding.

It ends.

He had not known, at forty-two, that this was available.

---

Alex sat slightly outside the conversation with a coffee going cold.

He knew what he had always felt in this situation. He had catalogued it at eleven, at fourteen, at nineteen, at every parents' evening and every school gate and every one of the times his father had come into the Velum office. A hot, tight, contemptible, automatic wish that his father would say the sentence slightly differently.

He had heard the gap where the gesture went. Of course he had. He heard every one.

And nothing happened.

He waited for the flinch and it did not come, and in the space where the flinch had always been there was the other man, leaning forward with his elbows on his knees, listening.

And then it went, the way they went, sideways and complete and ninety seconds after he had stopped looking for it.

Because what the other man was listening to was a person who had arrived at thirty-seven in a country whose language ran at a speed he could not hold, with a wife and a trade in his hands that the country would not accept as a trade, and who had gone and got a job that started at eleven at night and a second job that started at seven in the morning, and had done both of them for ten years without once being late, and had in that decade learned an entire second country the way you learn a machine — from underneath, by taking the covers off, by finding out what the fifty-pence meter actually did and what the landlord could and could not legally do about it and which of the four men on the night shift would tell you the truth about overtime — and had come out of it able to rewire a flat and rebuild a gearbox and diagnose a broken web press by the sound it made through a wall, and had put a dead Compaq Presario on a kitchen table in front of an eight-year-old and said *this one's yours, it doesn't work*, which was the single most useful sentence anybody had ever said to Alex Wójcik, and had then gone out to work and left him to it for eleven weeks, and had never once come in to see how it was going.

His father was interesting.

He had spent twenty years hearing him as a mistake.

Ewa took a photograph of the three of them without asking, badly, at an angle. It is the photograph that would eventually sit on a shelf in a flat in Bow.

---

They got round to it because Nik asked, plainly, the way he had asked about everything else.

"So how is it going, the company?"

And Alex, who had said *it's great* to these people on the phone eleven days ago, said, "It's — there's a thing with my co-founder."

He told it badly, in the version that made everybody reasonable. The whiteboard. The compliance module. The twenty-two seconds of silence. The fact that Ben was a good man. The fact that there was nothing you could photograph.

Marek listened without moving.

At the end he said, "Is he a liar?"

"No. Genuinely no."

"Is he stupid?"

"He's the least stupid person I know."

"Mm." Marek looked at the fireplace with no fire in it. "So he is fast. And you are—"

He hunted for it, and did not find it in English, and used the Polish word. Alex translated it in his head as something between *thorough* and *slow*. It was accurate and it was not an insult.

"So. He is fast, and you are that. And the room goes to who is fast."

"Yes."

"Yes," said his father. "This I know."

He turned in the chair.

"Aleksander." He said it to the fireplace rather than to his son. "You don't have to be loud to be right."

Alex looked at the floor.

"That doesn't help, though," he said, more sharply than he meant to. "That's a nice thing to say, but the room *does* go to who's fast. Being right quietly is just being wrong slowly."

"I didn't finish," said Marek.

"Sorry."

His father took a while over it. He was not building to anything. He was looking for words in a language that had never quite fitted him.

"You say it one time," he said. "Slow. Then you stop, and you don't say it again."

A pause.

"If you say it one time and stop, they have to look at it. Three times, it is noise." He turned his hand over. "And if you don't say it, then it was never in the room. And after, you are angry with them. For a thing they never saw."

Two feet away, Nikhil Raghavan sat absolutely still.

"At the works I said nothing, six years," Marek went on, to nobody in particular. "Then the machine was broken."

The doors went, and let the car park in, and shut.

---

Nik went off to round three at twenty to two and lost, comprehensively, to a man he should have beaten, because he was not there.

Marek and Ewa and Alex had lunch in the restaurant, under a ceiling with a return vent in it that ran a full tone above the lobby. Ewa asked about the food four times. Marek asked about the compliance module and was told about it and understood roughly none of it and asked two further questions anyway.

And at the end, when Ewa had gone to find the toilets, Marek Wójcik said, looking at his son across a table:

"You are all right?"

And Alex said, "Yeah. Tired. It's a big year."

And his father — who had spent thirty-one years, four hours of sleep at a time, on exactly one thing in this world, and who had known since the moment he walked into that lobby that something was badly wrong with his son, and had watched him all morning, and counted the number of times he checked his phone, and heard the eleven days of *it's great* — looked at him for a second and a half.

"Good," he said. "Then we go and look at this canal your mother has read about."
