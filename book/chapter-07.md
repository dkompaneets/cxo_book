# 7. Post-Mortem

Round one began at seven, and by twenty past the atrium of The Ardenne had gone quiet in a way that no other sort of gathering manages.

It was not silence. There was the air handling. A chair. Somebody's stomach. Every ninety seconds or so the small plastic *tock* of a clock being pressed. But a hundred and seventy-six people had stopped talking at the same moment and stayed stopped, and the effect on a man arriving from open-plan offices and video calls and a three-year-old was roughly that of stepping into cold water.

Nik sat down at board thirty-one, set out his pieces, and felt his shoulders come down two inches.

This was the thing, and it was not the winning. He had tried to explain the winning to Meera once, four or five years ago, and had heard himself doing it about eleven words in, and had stopped.

The thing was that for the next four hours nobody was going to say *sorry?* Nobody was going to lean in. There was no accent in here, because there was nothing to have an accent in. His opponent was a boy of about nineteen with a fringe and a Lidl bag, and he was not going to form a view about Nikhil Raghavan's competence off the first four seconds of a sentence, because there would be no sentences. There would be a position, and the position would be true.

Entry, forty-eight pounds, seven rounds, call it four hours a round. Twelve pounds an hour of not explaining himself, against a nursery that charged nine-fifty and a therapist Meera had once suggested who charged ninety, and of the three it was comfortably the best value in the portfolio.

The boy had a digital clock that cost about thirty pounds and a set that had come out of a school cupboard. Nik had brought neither, because you never need to.

He shook hands. He played e4. The boy played c5.

---

It was not a good game.

He got a slight edge out of the opening, the way he usually did, because he had prepared and the boy had not. The boy was graded 1840 against Nik's 2005, a hundred and sixty-five points, about a seventy-two per cent expected score, and Nik had all three figures before either of them had castled. He always did. Twelve pounds an hour of not explaining himself, and the machine still came to the board and sat down beside him and got its pencil out.

Then on move seventeen the boy did something loose on the queenside — a pawn push that was, Nik saw immediately, over-ambitious and probably losing.

And Nik did not take it.

He looked at the position for twenty-eight minutes, which was a third of his clock, spent on a hundred and eighty pounds of prize fund he was not going to win, on a decision he had in some real sense already taken. There was a line, and he could see the first six moves of it, clean as a rail: open the centre, rook comes to d7, and it is not quite winning but it is extremely uncomfortable to be the boy. It required calculating a knight sacrifice four moves in.

He got to the fourth move and could not see the end of it.

What happened next was so familiar that he could have narrated it while it was going on.

He thought: *and if it doesn't work.*

Then a small piece of machinery turned over inside him and produced, as it had been producing for twenty years, the alternative. The solid move. Rook to c1. Nothing whatever wrong with it. It kept everything defended, and it kept the small edge, and it kept the position closed and safe and — this was the honest word, the one he used only in his own head — *unexposed.*

He played Rc1. Twenty-eight minutes of thought, and the move itself took four seconds, and the four seconds were the only part of the whole transaction that had ever been in doubt.

He won anyway. It took another fifty-one moves and was, by the end, mechanical. The boy overpressed on move forty-two out of frustration at not being able to get anything going, and dropped a pawn, and then another. He resigned in a dead position with a good grace that made Nik feel obscurely ashamed of himself.

"Well played," said the boy.

"You were fine. The pawn on move seventeen."

"I know. I knew as soon as I let go."

They shook hands. It was ten past eleven.

One point. On the prize table a point was worth about thirty pounds if you finished on five and a half and nothing whatever if you finished on five, which is the sort of distribution Nik dealt with all day and had never once found funny in his own accounts and found extremely funny here.

---

The analysis room was the Kenilworth, a function room off the atrium with a carpet from 1998 and fourteen tables of people replaying games at speed. There was a tea urn. There was a plate of biscuits the hotel had put out at seven, which had reached the state biscuits reach, and which Nik costed on the way past at about four pence each and not worth it at the price.

He took his score sheet in, because that is what you do.

At the far table an old man was sitting alone with a board already set up, moving pieces about with one hand and writing with the other. He looked up when Nik came in.

"Raghavan. Board thirty-one."

Nik stopped.

"You beat me," Nik said. "Two years ago. Round four."

"I know."

"You had a rook on the seventh and I—"

"You had a rook on the seventh," said Wilf Tanner, "and you offered a draw, and I refused it, and then I lost. Sit down."

Wilf Tanner was eighty-three. He had been a structural engineer in Coventry for forty-one years and had played tournament chess since 1958. He had the build of a very old man who has stayed exactly the same height all his life. His hands shook slightly, except when they were moving a piece, at which point they did not shake at all.

Nik sat down and set up. The set was a Jaques from about 1970, weighted, worth four hundred pounds on a good day. Nik knew that within a second of touching a rook and said nothing, because there is a kind of man who prices another man's chess set out loud, and Nik had spent twenty years not being him in public.

It took twenty minutes to get to move seventeen, and Wilf did not say a single thing on the way. He put the pieces where they had been, in silence, with his eyes on the board, and Nik found himself narrating a little, apologetically — "he'd been quite quick up to here" — and being met with nothing, which taught him quickly to stop.

At move seventeen Wilf stopped.

"There," he said.

"I know."

"What did you look at?"

"Knight takes on d5, and then if he recaptures, rook to d7."

"How long?"

"Twenty-eight minutes."

"And you played Rc1."

"I couldn't see the end of it."

Wilf nodded slowly. Then he did a thing that nobody in Nik's professional life had done in six years, which was to sit, and think about the answer, and not fill the gap with anything at all.

Thirty seconds went by. Someone at the next table said "no, no, no, *bishop*", and there was laughter.

"Show me," said Wilf. "Knight takes d5. Go on."

Nik played it. Wilf recaptured. Nik played Rd7, and Wilf found the defence, the only defence, the one that involves giving back the exchange, and Nik played the follow-up and Wilf played the second-only-defence. Eight moves into a line that Nik had not been able to see the end of at the board, they arrived at a position so plainly winning that neither of them said so.

"Ah," said Nik.

"Yes."

"I mean, at the board—"

"At the board you had twenty-eight minutes," Wilf said. "It took us four."

Nik looked at the position.

"Right," he said. "So I'm a coward."

He said it lightly, in the tone he used for things like this — the little self-deprecating shrug he had been deploying for two decades, which had never once failed to make an English person say *oh, no, not at all.*

Wilf did not say *oh, no, not at all.*

He put the pieces back to move seventeen and set the position out again neatly, and looked at it rather than at Nik.

"No," he said. "You're not a coward. A coward would have offered a draw." He straightened a rook a fraction. "You won the game. You'll win the next one too, probably, if you get white."

"But?"

"You've got the best defensive technique of anyone in this room under seventy," said Wilf. "Genuinely. It's a real thing, you've built it, it's taken years. And you use it in positions where there's nothing to defend."

Nik said nothing.

"That's not the seventeenth move," Wilf went on. "That's every game of yours I've ever seen. You play like a man guarding a door long after the room behind it has emptied out."

Somebody dropped a chess clock at the next table and there was a general noise.

Wilf reached for a biscuit, found it was one of the bad ones, and put it back.

"So what is it you think you're protecting?" he said.

And Nikhil Raghavan opened his mouth to answer.

It was the tone that did it. Asked kindly, with the head on one side, in the voice people use when they have decided you are a bit sad, and Nik would have had four answers ready and one of them funny. Asked as a challenge, and he would have had a different four.

But Wilf Tanner asked it the way he had said everything else. Flatly, as a technical question about a position. The manner of a man who has spent forty-one years asking *what is this column actually carrying*, and has learned that the answer is very often *nothing, and it has been in the way since 1974.*

He wanted the load path. He was not interested in Nik.

"I don't—" said Nik.

He stopped.

He sat in a function room called the Kenilworth with his hands flat on the table on either side of a chessboard, and went looking, and priced what he found. Nothing. No secret. No wound with a story attached that he could produce and be understood about. A door, and twenty years of standing in it, and behind it a room he could have let anybody walk through at any point since about 2004, at a cost of nothing.

"That's a very good question," he said at last, in the wrong voice. The board voice. The one that means *I'll come back to you on that.*

Wilf Tanner nodded, satisfied, and began putting the pieces away.

"Round two's at two," he said. "Don't eat the fish."
