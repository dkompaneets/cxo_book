# 4. The Exchange Rate

There is a kind of true that is not honest, and Nikhil Raghavan had made a career of it.

Lodestone had no boardroom. Gus Delaney believed boardrooms made people behave like a board, so the quarterly numbers were given in the games room, standing, at the end of a long table, eighteen inches from a plush rabbit. Behind the rabbit was a cabinet of trophies for games that no longer ran on any hardware you could buy. Nik had priced the cabinet once, on a slow Friday. Two hundred and sixty pounds, and about nine thousand pounds of shelf space at the Guildford rate, and he had never mentioned either figure to anybody, because there is no way to say that sentence and come out of it well.

Four numbers on the slide. Every one correct.

"Cash at quarter-end, eleven-two. Runway on current burn, fourteen months. That's before Sunward lands in November, and if it lands on time we're at nineteen."

"Nineteen months," said Gus, to the room, with satisfaction.

"If it lands on time," said Nik, and went to the next slide before anyone could weigh the *if*.

He had a line ready about the rabbit. He used it. Something about the only board member with tenure — and the video lag took the first three words, and what arrived in the room was a small noise with no shape on the end of it. Two people smiled a beat late, out of kindness, which is worse than nothing.

A joke that fails costs four seconds, which he had.

And about a tenth of a point of the room, which he did not.

So he did the thing he did. He put a number in front of the silence and let the number carry it, and by the second bullet the room had forgotten there had been a joke, and only one person in it knew there was anything to forget.

The unsaid version went like this. The eleven-two had two-point-four of publisher prepayments in it, and those came back if Sunward slipped past November. The runway assumed a headcount nine people short, and an open role lengthens a runway the way a skipped meal lengthens money. Sunward had been given three dates and had missed three dates.

None of it was a lie. All of it was in the pack, on page nineteen, in a table, correctly labelled, put there by Nik so that it would exist quietly.

He said *healthy* once, about cash. He said *we're comfortable* about the Q3 position. Two words in forty minutes. Each did the work of a small load-bearing wall.

Then Bridget spoke. Bridget had run a studio in Dundee in the nineties and was the only person present who had ever read an appendix.

"Nikhil. The clawback on Sunward. That's the thirtieth of November, is that right?"

Nik felt his whole body go still and warm, the way it does when the thing you have arranged to be invisible turns out to have been visible the entire time.

"That's right. Page nineteen."

"It's a big number."

"It is."

"But you're relaxed."

A second, maybe a second and a half. Long enough to say: *no, I've been awake at four in the morning since March, I put it at about even, and we should be talking to the publisher now and not in October.*

He looked at Gus on the screen. Gus, who had made a game about a rabbit, and had put his own house into this company in 2016, and had the rabbit behind him on a shelf, watching.

"I'm watchful," said Nik. "That's my job. But yes. Relaxed."

"Good," said Bridget. "Then I am."

Gus rang him afterwards, on the phone rather than the video, which he did about twice a year.

"That was excellent. Honestly. You just — you make it feel like there's a floor under everything. I don't know how you do it."

Nik looked at the car park.

"Practice," he said.

---

The rest of the day arrived as numbers, which was the only way anything arrived.

The bank renewed the Dublin overdraft forty basis points worse than last year, in a letter using the word *partnership* twice. Forty points on eight hundred thousand is three thousand two hundred a year. Not a catastrophe. Also a junior QA contractor for a month, and he had both before the second paragraph.

Priyesh sent a forecast revision. Twenty-eight, thorough, and he had found a marketing accrual sitting in the wrong quarter since March, eleven thousand four hundred of it. He presented this with the shining face of a man who has not yet discovered that being right about the accounts is a hobby and not a career. Nik told him it was good work, and meant it. He watched him leave lit up, and thought about how many years that lasts.

HR wanted the salary bands finalised by Friday. He could not do it. Finalising them meant telling nine people a number, and he had not decided which of two numbers to tell them. Across nine people the gap between the two was thirty-one thousand a year, which is one senior artist, or four hundred pounds a head, depending on which way round you needed it to sound.

Then he went out for a coffee, because he had eaten nothing, and stood in a queue in Pret behind a man explaining to somebody that the flat white here was inconsistent.

He got to the front. He asked for a flat white and a bag of the crisps with the black label.

And the girl at the till said: "Sorry?"

---

Nothing had happened. That was true of it either way. She was nineteen and there was a coffee grinder going and a man behind him talking about consistency, and she had not heard the second half of a sentence in a loud room, which is a thing that occurs four hundred times a day in that shop to people from Kent.

He said it again, slightly louder. She got it, and smiled. That was the whole event.

He paid three pounds forty and went and stood outside on the Guildford road with the coffee going cold in his hand, because he was nineteen years old and standing in a room in Croydon.

Five years of it, 1999 to about 2004. He would say a sentence, and there would be a beat, and the other person's face would do a small thing — a fractional leaning-in, nothing rude, nothing anybody could have been accused of — and then *sorry?* And he would say it again, and it would land in the room four seconds late and about ten per cent smaller than the sentences on either side of it.

Four seconds and ten per cent. Forty times a week. For five years.

He had totalled it once, at a desk, in a bad week, and got something over eleven thousand sentences. Then he had stopped, because that is the kind of sum a man does when the sum is not the point.

He tried going quiet first. That cost him a promotion in 2005 to a man called Duncan who was worse than him and had said four thousand things to Nik's sixty. The company was not wrong to do it, either, which he had understood at the time and had never stopped understanding.

So he tried the other thing.

And the other thing worked immediately, which was the disaster. You take a true thing and you press it. Not falsify. *Press*, the way you press a shirt. And it lands, and when it lands nobody leans in, and when nobody leans in nobody says *sorry?*, and the four seconds are gone.

Twenty-six years later a girl in a sandwich shop had not heard him over a grinder, and he had spent three pounds forty and a quarter of an hour of an afternoon standing on a pavement about it.

He put the coffee in a bin without drinking it. Three pounds forty, and he registered the waste, and there was not one single thing he could do about any of it.

---

The commute was fifty-one minutes on a good day and had been the honest hour of his life for four years, because nobody could reach him on the A3. After the Wisley interchange the phone lost signal for ninety seconds. He had come to look forward to those ninety seconds the way other men look forward to a drink.

He did the real arithmetic there. Not the studio's. His own.

Take-home after tax and pension, just under seven thousand a month. A number that sounds like the end of a problem and is the beginning of one.

Rent on the house in Woking, twenty-one fifty. A house and not a flat, three bedrooms, because Anaya needed a room and Rohan needed a room and Meera had asked for neither. Nik had decided them.

Anaya's school, eleven-forty a month, chosen when she was six and impossible to undo without explaining to a nine-year-old why she was leaving her friends.

Chennai, nine hundred in a normal month.

The car, the insurance, the nursery days, and a food shop that had climbed from a hundred and sixty a week to two hundred and thirty without anybody agreeing to it and without anybody eating noticeably better. Rohan alone was forty pounds a month, and ate, as far as Nik could establish, four things.

Four hundred pounds left in a good month. Not four hundred of savings. Four hundred of *slack.* The margin between a family and an event.

The boiler was eleven years old.

He was the chief financial officer of a company with eleven million pounds in the bank, and he was about seventeen hundred pounds short, and there was nobody alive he could say it to.

He checked it for self-pity every time, the way you check a bag for keys. It came back a fact about the shape of a life and nothing more. Tell Gus, and he was a CFO with money trouble, a phrase that ends a career regardless of the arithmetic under it. Tell Meera the real version, and she would go back to work with Rohan at three, and the nursery would cost more than the job paid, and she would do it anyway, and he would have to watch her do it.

Signal came back past Ripley. Eleven things arrived at once and he was grateful for all of them.

---

Meera was on the sofa with the baby monitor on the arm and her eyes shut, not asleep. Resting with the tension of a woman who expects to be needed in ten minutes.

"He went down at seven. He'll be up at nine, and again at one." She opened her eyes. "How was work?"

"Good. Busy. Gus is happy about Sunward."

"Is Sunward going to be fine?"

"Sunward's going to be fine."

She nodded slowly, and looked at him a beat longer than the answer needed, and said, "There's dal. It's not very good. Rohan put a car in it."

They ate. The car had been removed but had left an opinion.

Anaya came down in pyjamas to argue about a school trip. Nik said yes before he heard the price, then heard the price, then said yes again in a slightly different voice.

"It's fine if it's too much," said Anaya, who was nine and had radar.

"It isn't too much."

A hundred and forty-five pounds. Thirty-six per cent of the slack. He had the percentage before she was out of the room, and hated having it, and had it.

---

The maroon suitcase came down off the wardrobe on the Wednesday with a smell in it that was thirty years old.

There had been two. Bought on the Mount Road in the August of 1999, cheap hard shells, one grey and one maroon, three hundred and forty rupees the pair, which his mother had negotiated over eleven minutes and had described afterwards to four separate people. The grey one died in 2014. This one had a shell scratched matte, a corner crushed by a baggage system in Frankfurt in 2009, and a front left wheel so worn that the whole case pulled to one side and had to be leaned against, like a difficult dog.

Inside the lid, a fabric name tape, sewn by hand. A woman at the temple had told Shobha Raghavan that bags get swapped at Heathrow. No source, no mechanism, no evidence. It had nonetheless determined the interior fittings of the case that would follow her son for twenty-six years.

His father had come to the airport in the good shirt and said very little. At the gate he pushed an envelope down into Nik's shirt pocket — into the pocket, not the hand, so it could not be given back — and put his palm flat over it, and said, in Tamil:

*"Don't call for money. Call for anything else."*

He had never once called for money. He had also never called for anything else, but the instruction had only covered the one.

Anaya sat on the end of the bed with her chin on her fists and watched him fold shirts.

"Appa. Why don't you just buy a new one."

"There's nothing wrong with this one."

"The wheel's broken."

"The wheel isn't broken. The wheel is worn."

"That's the same thing." She got up and pulled it two feet across the carpet and it went sideways into the wardrobe door. "See. In Dubai they have suitcases with four wheels. You just push them. They go anywhere."

"When have you been to Dubai?"

"Sofia went to Dubai."

"Ah," said Nik. "Sofia."

She flumped back onto the bed. "It's not even a nice colour."

And he opened his mouth to tell her — *this case came here with me, my mother sewed that tape in with her hands, at a table, in a flat you have never seen* — and heard in advance exactly how it would land. The immigrant father. The parable. The story with a moral on the end, handed to a girl in a school uniform who has been asked to feel something on cue. He had done it twice. He had watched her face do the small polite arrangement both times.

"It's maroon," he said. "It's a very good colour. Go and do your reading."

"It's *brown*," said Anaya from the door, having got the last word, which was fair. She had won on the merits.

He stood in the bedroom holding a shirt for a while.

Then he went down and moved seventeen hundred pounds to Chennai out of the account he was not supposed to use for it, and typed in the reference box, as he did every month, the word *ROUTINE* — so that if Meera ever went through the statement it would sit in the column with all the others and not put its hand up.

---

At eleven, the house quiet and Rohan not yet awake, he sat at the kitchen table with the laptop and did not open the family budget.

He opened the registration page for the Midlands Open. Five days at a hotel outside Birmingham called The Ardenne. Seven rounds across a long weekend. Entry forty-eight pounds.

He had played it twice. It was the only thing in his year he did not exaggerate about in either direction. He did not tell people at work that he played chess, because he could not have borne their finding it charming. He did not tell his mother he was going, because she would tell the neighbours, and by the second telling he would be in a tournament in London, and by the fourth he would be winning it.

The form wanted a name. The box said FULL NAME AS IT WILL APPEAR ON THE PAIRING SHEET.

Nik sat with his hands over the keys. Then he typed the whole thing, slowly.

*Nikhil Raghavan.*

Nobody had called him Nikhil at work in six years. He had shortened it himself in 2018, in a meeting in Reading, where a man had said "Nick? Nick-hill? Sorry, mate—" and Nik had said "Nik's fine," and it had been fine.

It cost so little at the moment of the transaction that he had not noticed the transaction. He had thought about it perhaps a thousand times since. There is no exchange rate for that. He had looked.

He paid the forty-eight pounds. Two point four per cent of a month's slack, and the best value in the portfolio.

Then he set up a position on the board in his head — the one he had lost to an old man two years ago from a winning position, because he had spent forty minutes making sure he could not lose — and played it through, both sides, in silence, until a small voice upstairs began to complain about the dark.
