# 18. That's Nice, Nik

Sunday was a double-round day, and Nikhil Raghavan won both of them.

He won round four in the morning against a very solid man from Nuneaton who had beaten him in 2023, and he won it with a piece sacrifice on move twenty-two that he had not calculated. That was the part he kept coming back to. He had sat there with eleven minutes on his clock and had seen four moves of it, and had thought *and if it doesn't work* — and had then, for the first time in about six years, thought: *then it doesn't work.*

It worked. It was worth one point. The point was worth nothing, and he sat looking at the position for a while anyway.

He won round five in the afternoon in eighty minutes, against a junior who played the opening off a video and had no idea at all what to do when it ran out. There is a particular expression on the face of a fifteen-year-old at the exact move his preparation stops, and Nik, who had worn that expression himself in three currencies and on two continents, enjoyed it a good deal more than he was proud of.

Between the two he had a jacket potato in the atrium café with Wilf Tanner, who had drawn both his games and was disgusted about it. The potato was six pounds fifty and had been under a lamp since about eleven. Nik ate all of it.

"Two draws," said Wilf, in the voice of a man reporting a bereavement.

"They happen."

"They happen to other people." He said four things about the Nuneaton game, all of them technical, and one about the sacrifice, which was: "You'd have played Rc1 on Thursday."

"I know."

"Mm," said Wilf, and ate his soup.

Walking out of the playing hall at the end of the afternoon, into a corridor with a window in it, Nik discovered that he was — the word arrived, and he inspected it and turned it over and found no fault in it — *happy.* That he had been happy for most of a day. And that this had last happened on some date he could not produce.

He rang home at six.

---

Meera picked up on the fourth ring with a noise behind her that was Rohan doing something to a saucepan.

"Hi. Hang on. *Rohan.* Hi."

"Is it a bad time?"

"It's six o'clock. It's always a bad time. How's the chess?"

And Nikhil Raghavan, standing in a corridor with a window in it, at the end of the best day he had had in a year, opened his mouth and heard himself say:

"Really good, actually. I won both. I'm in the top group now."

There is no top group at the Midlands Open. There is one Swiss, and after five rounds Nik was on four out of five, which put him equal second on the live standings on a laptop by the arbiter's desk. Equal second is a true and excellent thing and it takes seven words.

"That's brilliant. Wait — both? In one day?"

"Two rounds today. And the morning one was against a guy who's beaten me before, and he's — I mean, he's rated about two hundred points above where I am."

He was rated forty points above. A hundred and sixty points, added in the half-second between one clause and the next, at no cost, the way a figure gets rounded up in a room where nobody has the source data.

"So that's a proper scalp. Nik, that's amazing."

"And I've been playing well. Like — actually well. Attacking." That part was true. "The hotel's a bit grim, but there's a decent restaurant and I've been going to bed at eleven, so I've had about thirty-five hours of sleep in four days."

Four and a half a night. Up until one on the Thursday with a boy at a chessboard, and half past twelve on the Friday, which is eighteen hours across four nights against the thirty-five he had just put in the air. The restaurant was fine. He had eaten in it once, for nineteen pounds, and had afterwards thought about the nineteen pounds for considerably longer than he had thought about the meal.

"You sound completely different."

"I feel completely different."

"Good." A crash somewhere. "*Rohan.* — Sorry. And there's people to talk to? You're not just sitting in the room?"

And here it came.

"Yeah, no, it's been really social. There's a whole crowd of us — there's an old boy who's a retired engineer, he's fantastic, and I've been having dinner with a group of them, and there's a guy who's the CTO of a security startup, and a woman who's a CEO, and this COO from one of the big banks. We all went up on the roof on Friday and stayed up there half the night."

Sentence by sentence, true.

Said in that order, in that voice, it described a different weekend belonging to a different man. A weekend of dinners and a crowd, rather than one jacket potato, one accidental roof, and two conversations that had gone somewhere he had not intended.

"Wow," said Meera. "Look at you."

"I know."

"That's nice, Nik."

---

He did not notice for about four seconds.

He was already going on — something about the standings, something about Tuesday — and there was a delay, the way there is when you walk past a shop you used to know. Then the sentence came back round and hit him in the side of the head.

*That's nice, Nik.*

Flat. Warm. Not cold, no edge on it anywhere. The exact tone in which you say *that's nice* to a four-year-old who has told you about a dream. Pleasant, and complete, and it asked nothing further.

And then, stacked up behind it like carriages, every other time.

*That's nice, Nik*, about the promotion in 2021.

*That's nice, Nik*, about the chief executive ringing him personally.

*That's nice, Nik*, four years ago, when he came home about the funding round, said while looking for a shoe.

She was tired. She was extremely tired, and he had built a good deal on it.

A tired woman asks *and then what happened.*

She had run the test a few hundred times — *is this the size he says it is* — and got the same answer, and stopped running it, without a decision and without a row.

"Nik?"

"Sorry. I'm here."

"You went quiet."

"Signal," said Nik.

"Right." A pause, and then, in a different voice, the real one, the one from before: "Are you all right? You sound—"

"I'm fine. I'm great. I'm going to go and get some food."

"OK. Well done today. Genuinely." And then, half off the phone: "*Rohan. Give it to Mummy.*"

She went.

---

He did not go and get food.

He went up to room 812 and sat at the little desk and opened *2020 household v3*, because he had promised himself in a kitchen in Woking that he would do it properly at the hotel, and because after twenty minutes in a corridor doing it properly seemed suddenly possible. A clean, cold, adult act.

He opened the credit card. He typed the number into the cell.

Nothing exploded. The twelve-month view at the bottom went from a small positive to a number in brackets, and *closing balance, month 12* turned red, because he had written the conditional formatting himself in 2020.

He looked at it for a minute.

Then he began to work.

He ran it four times.

**Run one.** As is. Chennai at nine hundred a month, plus one lakh sixty for the physiotherapy in August. Anaya's school at eleven-forty. Croatia in the last week of August, four thousand two hundred all in, booked in January, one instalment paid. Result: they ran out in the third week of September, the card went up by about three thousand, and the twelve-month closing sat in brackets at five figures.

**Run two.** Cancel Croatia. He got the cancellation terms up in another tab. Fifty per cent retained after the first of July, so cancelling recovered two thousand one hundred pounds and cost the family the only week of the year in which Meera did not do bedtime alone. It worked. It worked to the tune of a hundred and forty pounds a month of slack.

**Run three.** Keep Croatia, take Anaya out of the school. Eleven-forty a month, straight out, from September. The model went green so hard it was almost funny. He sat and looked at it, and wrote in the notes column, in the language of a professional: *state alternative available from Sept, wait list, mid-year entry.*

Then he sat and thought about the actual thing. A nine-year-old told in August that she will not be going back to where Sofia and Nell and the whole of her life are, told it by a father who earns a hundred and eleven thousand pounds a year.

**Run four.** Cut Chennai. Nine hundred to four hundred, no physiotherapy package, which meant the government hospital, which meant nine months on a knee already stopping his father walking to the end of the road. He typed it in and looked at the green cells and sat back.

There was no version.

That was the finding, and he was a man who respected a finding. No arrangement of these numbers gave his father a knee, his daughter a school, and his wife one week. It did not matter how many times he ran it, because it was not a modelling problem. It was an arithmetic one. And he had been avoiding it since March in the way that only a man who is extremely good at arithmetic can avoid arithmetic.

He was the chief financial officer of a company with eleven million pounds in the bank. Nine people had listened to him on a call ten days ago and gone away reassured.

It was ten to nine on a Sunday evening and he could not afford his own life.

---

The message came at 8:56.

*Meera: The school payment's been declined. They've emailed. It says the direct debit was returned on Friday and there's a £25 charge and it has to be cleared by Thursday or she can't start the term. It's £1,140.*

*Meera: Nik there isn't £1,140 in the joint account. There's £310.*

*Meera: What's going on?*

He did not move.

The transfer had gone on the Wednesday. Seventeen hundred pounds, to Chennai, out of the joint account instead of the other one, because he had done it standing in a kitchen at eleven at night, in the app, on the phone, with the reference typed in as *ROUTINE.*

Three hundred and ten pounds.

He did not convert it into anything. He noticed that distantly, the way a man notices a noise stopping. The machine had run every figure he had met for twenty-six years, and it looked at this one and had nothing to say about it at all.

Nikhil Raghavan sat at the desk in room 812 of The Ardenne, with a spreadsheet in front of him in which the numbers had at some point stopped being numbers, and a phone in his hand, and a suitcase against the wall with a name tape sewn into the lid.

Outside the window the car park, and the ring road, and the aircraft going round.

Somewhere below him, four floors down, a hundred and seventy-six people were putting the pieces back on the boards for the morning.
