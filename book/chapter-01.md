# 1. Twelve Minutes

The calendar at Bellwether ran in twelve-minute blocks.

Kate Merrick gave a reason for this when asked. The medieval English penny divided into twelve. A unit you could split three ways or four and have nothing left over. People liked the reason. It was charming and it was true and it was not why.

Here is why. A half-hour meeting takes half an hour. A twelve-minute meeting is over before anyone has finished being pleased to see you.

**9:24, Monday.** She was in a room she had no business being in.

Sprint review, returns platform, four levels below her. Second floor, the small room with three glass walls and a print of a whale on the fourth. Everyone called it the Aquarium. Nine people at a table built for eight.

She came in at 9:23 and the room did the thing rooms did. The half-second rearrangement. The tiny weather change of a floor discovering the chief executive has joined the sprint review. A young man called Otis stood up to give her his chair.

"No. No, honestly." She took it.

Priyanka from engineering was walking them through queue depth on the refund service. Kate watched a dashboard on her phone under the table.

Twelve numbers, black background. They had built it for her in the first year, when Bellwether was two brands and a warehouse in Erith. Ten green. One amber, which meant nothing — a contractor set that threshold in 2021 and set it slightly wrong, and every quarter somebody said they should fix it, and every quarter it stayed.

One red.

She looked at the red number for two seconds. Two seconds was as long as she looked at anything.

"Sorry." The room stopped. "Sorry. Go back one. The four hundred and twelve. Is that refunds queued, or customers?"

"Refunds."

"So some are the same person."

"Some are the same person."

"How many?"

The pause. She caused it forty times a day and she knew its shape from the inside: a competent person arriving at the exact edge of what they had prepared.

"I'd have to check."

"It'll be one-point-four. It's always one-point-four." Kate was already standing. "So we're annoying two hundred and ninety people, not four hundred. Different meeting. Tom, six minutes at eleven. Not you, Gareth, you've got the board thing."

Gareth Pryce, chief technology officer, two chairs down, asked nothing all meeting, said: "Sure."

Out of the Aquarium at 9:31. Three minutes over. Three minutes over for the rest of the day.

---

The corridor was the only place she was ever alone. She had learned to use it.

Phone at her mouth. Walking.

"Dawn. Hi. Find me twelve minutes with Tom before eleven, and if it has to eat the Ocean Group call then push Ocean to Thursday and tell them it's because I want to give it proper time, which is true. Also twenty with Harriet before the summit. I know there isn't any." At the lift. "So find it."

Sent. On the second floor a phone buzzed beside a photograph of two children on a beach, in weather that was clearly not warm.

**10:52.** Tom Ferreira's part of the floor. She stood at his desk rather than sat. Standing kept it short.

Tom was VP of Engineering. Gareth was CTO. On the org chart a clean line ran from Kate to Gareth and from Gareth to Tom. In the physical world Kate walked past Gareth's office, which had a glass door with Gareth behind it, to get to Tom's desk.

"Talk to me about the refund queue."

He talked to her about the refund queue. He was good. Better at this than Gareth, she thought, and put the thought where she kept the others like it.

Nine minutes and they had it. A retry policy doing exactly what it had been told to do, told the wrong thing eighteen months ago by somebody who no longer worked here.

"Ship it today."

"Gareth's got a freeze on until after the summit."

"Ship it today. I'll tell Gareth."

She did not tell Gareth. She meant to. It went on the list she kept in her head of things she would do when a gap came, a list with the structure and permanence of geology.

---

**7:02, Wednesday.** Fourth bullet of a status email. Heading: *Refill — update.*

*Following the payments review on 26/6, Refill launch moves to 9 September to accommodate the migration to Marrowbone. Comms updated. No change to Q4 forecast.*

She read it twice, standing in the kitchen, no coffee made.

Not the five weeks. The date. *The payments review on 26/6.* A review had happened. A real one, with minutes in it and a decision in it, and she was finding out now, in a bullet, because to the nine people in that room there had been nothing to tell.

She had been in Rotterdam on the twenty-sixth of June.

By 7:20 she had established that the decision was right, and that was the part that frightened her. Marrowbone was better. The incumbent had failed twice in June. Their tokenisation would be a problem in Germany by spring. Gareth ran it, Tom sat in it, the analysis was sound.

And it moved the biggest thing Bellwether was doing this year by five weeks, and nobody walked it past the person who owned the number it landed on, because owning the number was not on anyone's job description except hers.

She stood at the island. Her weight went onto the ball of her left foot. The foot turned out a few degrees, heel down.

Her body did this four times a year. At sinks. In lifts. On station platforms. She never caught it until after.

She caught it. She stopped. She made the coffee.

---

She had danced until she was eleven.

Tuesdays and Saturdays, a church hall in Amersham that smelled of hymn books and radiators. A Russian woman called Mrs Volkova who shouted and smelled of hairspray. Kate somewhere in the middle of the class — good, she thought, though there was nobody left to ask — and in the second half, when her body stopped asking permission.

One dinner she still had whole, the way you keep a photograph you did not choose to take.

She was nine. She came to the table with a certificate. A real one, distinction, her name in a teacher's italic. Her mother read it and was delighted, genuinely, warmly, the whole table lifting a degree with the pleasure of it.

"Katherine. That's wonderful."

And then, still smiling, in the same breath, reaching for the water: "And how's the maths coming? Because it's the maths that's the mountain, isn't it."

There was no cruelty in it. Kate spent four sessions with a therapist in 2019 defending exactly that — *they were warm, they were interested, nobody was ever cold* — in a voice she could hear going too fast. Her mother had not withheld praise. She had moved the line the moment Kate reached it, without malice, because in that house the line was not a place you arrived at. It was a direction you faced.

The Saturday class went first, for the eleven-plus tutor. The tutor went well. Then the scholarship. The scholarship wanted maths and maths was Tuesday.

Nobody stopped her dancing. There was no scene. There was a diary, and a list of what mattered, and dancing slid quietly down it, and one afternoon in March she noticed she had not been in five weeks and that nobody in the house had remarked on it.

She had not been to a class since. She was forty-one. She could still, at a kitchen island at twenty past seven in the morning, feel exactly where her weight ought to be.

---

**9:15.** Gareth in her office. She did it properly, because she had learned to do it properly.

"The decision's right." She meant it. "The analysis is good. I'd have made the same call. I'm not asking whether it's real. I'm asking who decided that five weeks of Refill was the price, and whether they knew what Refill was worth."

"It came out of the review, Kate. It's the migration window, we can't—"

"I know we can't. That's not the question." She heard herself. She did not stop. "Nine people moved the biggest thing we do this year. Not one of them owns the number it lands on. Nobody thought that was worth a phone call."

Gareth Pryce was forty-eight and had been a CTO at two companies before this one and did not deserve the temperature in the room.

"Then the process—"

"The process is fine. Nobody did anything wrong."

True. The most honest sentence she would say all week. Gareth heard it as a threat, and was right to.

After he left she stood at the window. Then she sat down, opened the operating rhythm document, and did the thing.

She added herself to the weekly platform sequencing call. Gareth's call, Tuesdays, forty minutes.

Then a new item. A Thursday half-hour. Any decision that moved a date on a top-ten initiative got walked past her first.

She typed the second one and felt a specific physical relief. A strap pulled tight.

At the bottom she added: *Temporary — through Q4.*

She believed it. She had believed the identical line in November, about the pricing forum, which she still chaired.

Dawn's reply came in four minutes.

*That's two more standing commitments in a week where you asked me to find twenty minutes with Harriet and I took it out of your lunch. Saying it out loud.*

*Noted*, Kate wrote. *Do it anyway.*

*Doing it.*

**14:00.** Harriet Nash rang about the summit agenda, and in the last ninety seconds, in the voice she kept for weather and for warnings: "Everyone's terribly excited about Refill for the August peak."

There was the door.

Two weeks to the summit. A chief executive could have said, calmly, on a Wednesday afternoon: *it's moved to September, here's why, it's the right call — and while I've got you, twenty-two isn't going to happen, and I'd like to do that properly, with a plan, not badly, in a corridor.*

"There's a payments migration in front of it," Kate said. "I'll walk the board through the sequencing at the summit."

"Lovely," said Harriet. "You're a machine."

---

**12:36, Thursday.** A car going east. She recorded a voice message to Sasha and Bea.

She was good at these. Voice messages were the only communication left with any of her actual voice in it, and she spent them like a currency about to be devalued.

"Right. So. I know. I *know.* Don't say anything, I can hear you both saying it. Here's the thing about Thursday." A lorry pulled out and Vasyl made a small noise of professional contempt. "The summit runs Thursday to Sunday, the board join at the end, Harriet wants a pre-read a week out, so the whole thing has to be finished by the fifth, which means Thursday is—"

She stopped. She rewound nothing. The message kept running.

"Look. I'm not going to make Thursday. I'll make the next one. Actually don't book the next one around me. Book it and I'll come, and if I don't come you're allowed to be properly angry rather than the nice kind of angry you both do, which is worse. I love you. I'm the worst. Bye."

She listened to it back before sending, which she never did with work messages. Then she sent it.

The replies came before the Blackwall Tunnel. Sasha sent a GIF of a woman sighing. Bea sent: *third time K.*

Then, four minutes later, because Bea was constitutionally unable to leave a thing sharp: *joking. come whenever. love you.*

Kate looked at *third time K.* A notification came in over the top of it about the Ocean Group. By the time she surfaced they were at the warehouse.

---

The problem she had not solved, two weeks away, went like this.

Bellwether had grown twenty-two per cent a year for four years. The board had accordingly written down a number for next year that assumed twenty-two per cent again. The number was not achievable. Kate knew it was not achievable and had known since March.

Three ways to say so.

Early. Which looks like managing expectations, which is the phrase people use to describe a chief executive beginning to fail.

Late. Worse.

Or bring a plan so obviously right that the number changes shape while everyone is looking at the plan.

She did not have the plan. She had four days of work stacked behind the summit, a folder of half-built versions, and the growing unfamiliar sickening sense that this was a problem she could not outwork.

She had never once, in twenty years, failed to outwork a problem.

---

**17:02.** Dawn Fowler put her coat on.

Kate saw it from across the floor and something in her went very still, the way it did four times a year.

Dawn packed her bag. A laptop she would not open. A lunchbox. A second lunchbox. The second lunchbox went onto Kate's desk on the way past.

"That's the aubergine thing. You said you liked it. It's better the second day, so don't eat it tonight, eat it tomorrow, which you won't."

"I'll eat it tomorrow."

"You'll eat it at eleven o'clock tonight standing up." Dawn was already turning. "Marcus has got football and Elsie's got a thing where I have to buy a shoebox. Not shoes. The box." She stopped at the top of the stairs. "The Harriet twenty minutes. I've got you the eighth, eight-forty. She's doing it from a car."

"That's fine."

"It's not fine, it's a car. But it's twenty minutes." Dawn looked at her for slightly longer than the exchange required. "Are you all right?"

"Fine," said Kate. "Big fortnight."

"Big fortnight," Dawn agreed, and went down the stairs, and out through the doors, and away into a life with a shoebox in it.

Kate stood at the window and watched the top of Dawn's head cross the car park, and had a thought so unwelcome she put it away before finishing it. Something about how one of them was leaving and one of them was staying, and only one of them had a reason that would survive being said out loud.

Then the phone went. Ocean Group. The thought was gone.

---

**21:40.** The cleaners came out of the lift with the trolley. The woman whose name Kate had asked for twice and lost twice said "All right?" and Kate said "All right," and the vacuum started up at the far end of the floor like weather coming in.

The tupperware sat unopened. At 22:20 she ate it standing up.

She read the version of the plan she had written on Sunday and thought was good. It was not good. It was a way of saying *we will try harder*, dressed up. She deleted a slide.

She wrote a note to herself in the doc — *the number is wrong, say the number is wrong* — and deleted that too. Documents in a company are not private. And she was not ready to have written it down.

**23:14.** She opened next week's calendar and looked at it the way you look at a wall.

She typed: *Dawn — sorry, I know. Can we get Ocean and the ops review into next week somewhere.*

She deleted *sorry, I know.* It was eleven at night and an apology would only make Dawn feel she had to reply.

She sent: *Dawn — Ocean and the ops review need to be next week. Anywhere.*

Delivered. Nobody read it. The person it was sent to was asleep in a house with a shoebox in it.

Kate put the phone face down.

Forty seconds later she turned it back over.
